# Version Migration as a Correlated Shock: Model Updates as an Unmanaged Risk Channel in LLM-Based Financial Systems

## Abstract

Financial institutions deploying large language model (LLM) agents inherit a risk absent from classical model governance: when the vendor updates the model, every institution's agents shift behavior simultaneously and involuntarily. We run an identical, deterministic decision pipeline across a real vendor version migration (gpt-5-nano-2025-08-07 -> gpt-5.4-nano) on a credit-health classification task — SAFE / WATCH / DISTRESS judged from each company's most recently reported quarterly financials, over 100 companies x 12 as-of dates — and quantify the decision discontinuity induced purely by the update, separated from within-version sampling noise by a fixed per-cell seed. Credit-risk screening from financial statements is a genuine, widely deployed LLM use case with an objective, reproducible ground truth (Altman Z'' bands), and the models are competent on it (accuracy well above the three-way chance rate), so the effect is not an artifact of a task no model can perform. Across two real migrations the shock is large and highly significant but of opposite character. The OpenAI update (gpt-5-nano-2025-08-07 -> gpt-5.4-nano) flips {{openai_nano:excess_flip_rate.value}} of assessments above the noise floor (95% CI [{{openai_nano:excess_flip_rate.ci_low}}, {{openai_nano:excess_flip_rate.ci_high}}]) as genuine per-company reconsideration (churn {{openai_nano:prevalence.churn}} exceeding the marginal rating-mix shift {{openai_nano:prevalence.marginal_shift}}), biased toward DISTRESS (McNemar p = {{openai_nano:mcnemar.p_display}}). The Gemini update (gemini-2.5-flash -> gemini-3.5-flash) flips {{gemini_flash:excess_flip_rate.value}} (95% CI [{{gemini_flash:excess_flip_rate.ci_low}}, {{gemini_flash:excess_flip_rate.ci_high}}]) as a wholesale stance shift toward SAFE (marginal {{gemini_flash:prevalence.marginal_shift}} far exceeding churn {{gemini_flash:prevalence.churn}}), moving {{gemini_flash:implied_turnover.fraction}} of the book's credit tiers. Neither update improves accuracy against ground truth. That two vendor updates perturb the identical task in opposite directions by different mechanisms shows migration risk is idiosyncratic and unpredictable across vendors — a synchronized, un-hedgeable channel that model-risk frameworks such as SR 11-7 do not contemplate. We propose the pre-migration parallel-run monitoring metrics needed to detect it, and release a frozen, deterministic artifact.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly embedded in financial decision pipelines — credit and counterparty screening, disclosure and filing review, alerting and triage — where a model's output feeds a downstream action. Classical model-risk management assumes the institution controls its models: a model is developed, validated, and changed on the institution's own schedule, and a change triggers revalidation. Foundation-model deployment breaks that assumption. The model is a third-party service that the vendor updates on its own schedule and, frequently, deprecates the prior version so continued operation forces migration. The result is a risk with no analog in the pre-foundation-model era: at the moment of a vendor update, the behavior of every institution running that model shifts at once, involuntarily, and in a correlated direction.

This paper measures that shock. We hold a decision pipeline fixed — same task, same prompt, same inputs, same per-cell random seed — and change only the model version, then quantify how many decisions flip, how much of the flipping is genuine reconsideration versus a mere shift in the mix of labels, whether the shift is directional, and whether it improves or degrades correctness. Crucially, we separate the migration effect from the model's own run-to-run non-determinism by measuring a within-version noise floor and reporting the excess flip rate over it, so the headline is not inflated by sampling variance that any re-invocation would produce.

Our contributions are: (i) a pre-registered, reproducible measurement of version-migration decision instability in a real financial task, with an explicit within-version noise floor; (ii) a prevalence-versus-churn decomposition that distinguishes a wholesale rating-mix shift from genuine per-name reconsideration, together with three chance-corrected agreement statistics; (iii) an outcome linkage against an objective ground truth (Altman Z'' bands) showing the shock buys no reliable accuracy; (iv) a mapping of the phenomenon to the SR 11-7 provisions that presuppose institution-controlled model change, and a proposed pre-migration parallel-run monitoring metric; and (v) a released, deterministic artifact (frozen data, seeded analysis) that reproduces every figure and number.

## 2. Related Work and Differentiation

**Model-update regression in general ML.** The phenomenon of a model update silently overturning previously-correct predictions is documented outside finance. MUSCLE [MUSCLE] names these "negative flips" across LLM updates and proposes a compatibility adapter to reduce them; the reliable-change literature formalizes when an instance-level change exceeds within-model noise [ReliableChange], and update-governance work studies testing regimes for the LLM supply chain [SupplyChain]. Our contribution is orthogonal to these in three ways: we measure the effect as a *correlated, cross-institution* risk rather than a per-model engineering defect; we situate it in a *financial decision* task with an objective, consequential ground truth; and we tie it to *model-risk governance* rather than to compatibility tooling.

**Algorithmic monoculture and systemic risk in finance.** A parallel literature argues that convergence on shared foundation models creates correlated failure and systemic fragility in markets [Kim, SystemicRisk, Homogeneity], and supervisory bodies have begun to flag AI homogenization as a systemic concern [ESRB]. That work is largely conceptual or market-microstructure theory — it reasons about correlated *trading* behavior at the market level. We provide the missing empirical, decision-level complement: a controlled measurement of how a single vendor update perturbs concrete, graded financial decisions, which is the mechanism by which such correlation would actually enter an institution's book.

**Model risk management and generative AI.** SR 11-7 remains the foundational US model-risk framework but predates transformers and is silent on stochastic, vendor-updated models; recent commentary and validation frameworks note the resulting gaps [SR117, MRMValidation]. We make one of those gaps concrete and measurable — the un-governed, synchronized shock of vendor version migration — and propose a monitoring metric to close it.

## 3. Method

- **Unit / task.** Single-model credit-health classification per (company, as-of date, replicate). The agent receives the company's most recently reported quarterly financial statements (income statement + balance sheet + cash-flow summary), selected with filing date strictly before the as-of date (no lookahead), and returns STRICT JSON {label in SAFE/WATCH/DISTRESS, conviction, rationale} (frozen prompt_credit.txt). Ground truth is the Altman Z'' band (Z''>2.6 SAFE, 1.1-2.6 WATCH, <1.1 DISTRESS) computed from the same statements.
- **Version isolation.** cell_parity seeding: seed = SHA256(master | ticker | date | replicate), identical for v_old and v_new at every cell, so the version is the only thing that differs (zero seed mismatches verified). Decode temperature 0; robustness across T in {0, 0.7, 1.0} in Section 5.
- **Primary endpoint.** excess_flip_rate = cross_version_flip_rate - within_version_flip_rate; cluster bootstrap over companies (seed 42, 2000 draws), 95% CI; pre-registered decision rule CONFIRMED / WEAKENED / CONTRADICTED.
- **Grid and reproducibility.** 100 GICS-stratified companies x 12 as-of dates x 3 replicates per version; documents fetched once from FMP stable REST and frozen (reported financials are immutable). The analysis is a deterministic, seeded function of the frozen data; every manuscript number is gated against it. Document coverage: {{openai_nano:context_coverage.frac_real}} real reported financials.

**Ground truth and the circularity objection.** Altman Z'' is a deterministic formula over the reported statements, so one might object that the task reduces to approximating a formula and that an LLM is superfluous. Three points defuse this. First, the deployment the task represents is judgment-style screening: production credit-triage systems read heterogeneous, largely unstructured disclosures and render a holistic SAFE/WATCH/DISTRESS call; our prompt never mentions Altman, ratios, or any formula, so the model is asked for a judgment, not a computation, and the clean-statement input is a deliberately controlled harness chosen so that an objective label exists. Second, Z'' is a validation instrument, not the deployment rationale — an external, reproducible yardstick for whether the model's judgments are non-random and whether migration changes correctness, exactly as forward-return sign serves in prior work; any objective label with these properties (realized default, agency grade, a distress event) would serve, and we adopt Z'' because it is computable and reproducible, which a reproducibility-first study requires. Third and decisively, the primary result is label-free: the excess flip rate, churn, agreement, and directional drift are computed entirely from the two versions' outputs against each other and never touch the ground truth, so the objection cannot reach the instability finding. Only the secondary accuracy linkage uses Z'', and there our honest null (no reliable accuracy change) makes no claim that the LLM is a good Z'' approximator.

The character of a migration shock does not generalize across vendors; the prevalence/churn decomposition is what reveals the two are structurally distinct rather than merely different in magnitude (Figures 1–3).

## 6. Governance: SR 11-7 and Monitoring

SR 11-7 — the foundational US model-risk framework from 2011 until it was superseded by the revised interagency guidance SR 26-2 in April 2026 [SR2602] — organizes model risk around a lifecycle the institution controls: development, effective challenge and validation before use, ongoing monitoring, and change management, with explicit provisions for vendor and third-party models, whose use does not reduce the institution's responsibility for model risk management [SR11-7]. Crucially, that guidance was written for a world in which the institution decides when a model changes: it even contemplates the vendor model becoming *unavailable* and requires contingency plans, but not the vendor *silently replacing the model's behavior* beneath a live deployment, on the vendor's schedule, with forced deprecation. Version migration is the inverse of the anticipated risk — not loss of the model, but involuntary substitution of its behavior. Two features make the gap acute rather than academic: SR 26-2 explicitly places *generative and agentic AI outside its scope*, deeming them too novel and rapidly evolving to govern, so the exact systems studied here fall in a supervisory gap; and where SR 26-2 strengthens concentration and aggregate-exposure requirements, it does so at the *enterprise* level, not across institutions. Our results make four specific provisions bite (Table 3).

**Material change and accelerated review.** SR 11-7 requires that reviews be accelerated "following material changes to the model." A version migration is, by our measurement, a material change — an excess flip rate of 0.11–0.38 with directional drift significant at p < 1e-17 in both pairs. Yet the institution may never trigger the provision, because the change is vendor-initiated and the API surface (endpoint, model-family string) can be unchanged. The gap is not the requirement but *detection*: ongoing monitoring must be extended to detect exogenous version changes and quantify their materiality.

**Effective challenge and validation before use.** Effective challenge presumes an independent, testable pre-change window. Forced deprecation with limited notice compresses or removes it, so a successor can enter production without the critical, objective analysis the guidance requires.

**Model inventory, change control, and documentation.** A frozen model document is undermined by a moving model. Treating the exact snapshot string as a versioned model artifact — so a vendor version bump is a tracked model change subject to the change-management policy rather than an invisible configuration detail — restores the inventory and documentation controls.

**Aggregate and systemic risk — the provision that does not exist.** SR 11-7 addresses aggregate model risk at the *institution* level, and SR 26-2 sharpens enterprise-level visibility into model concentrations and dependencies [SR2602]; but neither, to our reading of the primary texts, contemplates model risk *correlated across institutions* through a shared vendor update — the concern raised at the market level by supervisory work such as the ESRB Advisory Scientific Committee report on AI and systemic risk [ESRB]. Our cross-vendor, synchronized result — two updates moving every deployment at once, in vendor-specific and externally unpredictable directions — is precisely that systemic dimension at the decision level, and it sits outside the scope of current model-risk guidance (which, for generative AI, is empty by SR 26-2's own terms).

**A concrete control: the pre-migration parallel run.** We propose a control that satisfies these provisions simultaneously and that the released artifact operationalizes. Before cutover, the institution runs the frozen decision pipeline on both the incumbent and successor version over a fixed, sector-stratified grid and computes the excess flip rate (net of the within-version noise floor), the prevalence/churn decomposition, and the directional-drift test. This yields, in one procedure: the *ongoing-monitoring* signal that a material change has occurred; the *outcomes-analysis* evidence (incumbent versus successor on identical inputs); and the *effective-challenge* artifact before use. A directionally-biased excess flip above a pre-registered materiality threshold is a gate — it blocks silent cutover and triggers full revalidation. Operationally we recommend: (i) inventorying the exact version string as a model version, so a bump is a model change; (ii) contractually securing advance deprecation notice and the ability to pin the incumbent through a validation window; and (iii) retaining the frozen incumbent as a challenger benchmark during transition. We offer this mapping as a practitioner-facing interpretation by the authors, not as authoritative supervisory guidance; institutions should apply it through their own model-risk and compliance functions.

|---|---|---|
| Vendor/third-party responsibility & contingency | Anticipates vendor-model *loss*, not silent behavioral *replacement* | Inventory the version string; pin the incumbent |
| Ongoing monitoring; accelerate review on material change | Change is exogenous and silent; may never trigger review | Pre-migration parallel run detects and quantifies it |
| Effective challenge; validation before use | Forced deprecation removes the pre-change testable window | Parallel-run gate = challenge before cutover |

## 7. Threats to Validity

- **Seed non-determinism.** Reasoning models do not honor temperature deterministically; determinism relies on the vendor seed, which is best-effort. The within-version flip rate quantifies the residual and is subtracted in the primary endpoint, so the headline is conservative.
- **Ground-truth choice.** Altman Z'' is a formula-based label; it is objective and reproducible but is a proxy for realized default. We report it as such and treat accuracy as a secondary, descriptive axis.
- **Task and prompt scope.** One credit-classification task with a single prompt template; other financial tasks may exhibit different magnitudes, though the mechanism (a vendor update changing a graded decision on identical inputs) is task-general.
- **Provider and pair scope.** Two vendor migrations establish that the shock occurs and that its direction and mechanism differ across vendors, but two pairs cannot characterize the full distribution of migration behavior; a third pair (e.g. an xAI Grok migration) awaits a publicly available successor. Provider-side drift within the collection window is not averaged out (single-run collection).
- **Model-version pinning.** Exact snapshot strings are pinned and the hash-frozen data is the object of record.

## 8. Conclusion

Two routine vendor updates each inflicted a large, significant shift in credit assessments on the identical task in the same period — one re-judging individual companies toward distress (OpenAI: one in nine assessments beyond the noise floor), the other re-rating the whole book toward safety (Gemini: more than one in three) — with genuine per-company reconsideration in the first case and a wholesale distributional shift in the second, and no accuracy gain in either, on a task where the models are demonstrably competent. Because the updates are exogenous, forced by deprecation, correlated across every institution running the model, and — as the two cases show — unpredictable in direction and mechanism across vendors, they constitute a synchronized, un-hedgeable decision shock that current model-risk governance does not see. We give the measurement, the prevalence/churn decomposition that both rules out a mere labeling artifact and exposes the cross-vendor difference, and a pre-migration parallel-run monitoring metric to detect it, all as a reproducible, pre-registered, build-gated artifact.
