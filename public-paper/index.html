Article abstract

Financial institutions increasingly deploy large language models (LLMs) within credit and other decision-support workflows. However, vendor-driven model updates can alter behavior even when an institution holds its prompts, inputs, and downstream processes fixed. We quantify the decision instability associated with two prespecified model-version transitions: an OpenAI efficient-model update and a Google Gemini Flash update. Across both transitions, the experiment covers 100 companies, 12 analysis dates, and three replicates per version, producing 14,400 model calls. Identical inputs and matched per-cell seeds hold sampling conditions fixed, and within-version replicate disagreement provides an empirical noise floor. The primary endpoint is the excess cross-version flip rate above this floor. For the OpenAI migration, the cross-version disagreement rate exceeds the within-version noise floor by 11.2 percentage points (95% CI: 7.7–14.9); for the Gemini migration this excess is 38.1 percentage points (95% CI: 30.2–45.8). A prevalence–churn decomposition shows that the OpenAI update primarily reconsiders individual companies and shifts decisions toward greater severity, while the Gemini update produces a broad distributional shift toward the SAFE category. Neither migration shows clear improvement among observations whose labels change; the Gemini successor also shows lower aggregate class-balanced performance. Vendor version migration can therefore create material, directionally unpredictable decision discontinuities within otherwise unchanged LLM pipelines. We propose a pre-migration parallel-run control that measures excess flip rate, label-distribution shift, per-company churn, and directional drift before production cutover. The findings provide controlled evidence of deployment-level migration risk; broader cross-task and cross-institution effects remain for future study.

Measuring version-migration instability

1. What is the noise-adjusted decision flip rate after an LLM version migration?

In this controlled credit-health task, the excess flip rate was 11.2 percentage points for the OpenAI migration (95% company-clustered CI 7.7–14.9) and 38.1 points for the Gemini migration (30.2–45.8). These are results for two evaluated version pairs, not typical magnitudes for all LLM upgrades.

Evidence: § V-A; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

2. How can migration-induced model changes be separated from random response variation?

The study subtracts within-version replicate disagreement from cross-version label disagreement: excess flip rate = cross-version flip rate − pooled within-version noise floor. Repeated calls estimate ordinary response instability before attributing additional disagreement to the vendor migration.

Evidence: § III-B; Eqs. 3–6 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

3. What were the raw cross-version flip rates and within-version noise floors?

OpenAI had approximately 0.217 gross cross-version disagreement and a 0.105 pooled noise floor, yielding 0.112 excess. Gemini had 0.389 gross disagreement and a 0.008 noise floor, yielding 0.381 excess. Raw disagreement alone can conflate migration effects with ordinary endpoint variation.

Evidence: § V-A; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

4. Do the primary migration-instability estimates have confidence intervals excluding zero?

Yes, in the evaluated task. The company-clustered bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were [0.077, 0.149] for OpenAI and [0.302, 0.458] for Gemini. They quantify uncertainty in the task-level estimates, not systemic financial losses.

Evidence: § V-A; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

5. Does a more conservative noise-floor estimate eliminate the model migration effect?

No. Subtracting the greater of the two within-version noise floors yielded excess rates near 0.097 for OpenAI and 0.372 for Gemini. Both were positive, so the substantive finding was robust to that alternative. The pooled baseline remained the preregistered primary estimator.

Evidence: § III-B; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

Label distributions, directional drift and churn

6. Do different AI model upgrades shift financial risk labels in the same direction?

Not in the two tested migrations. The OpenAI successor predominantly reclassified individual companies toward greater credit-risk severity, while the Gemini successor strongly shifted classifications toward SAFE. These contrasting patterns are version-pair specific and are not a general judgment about either vendor.

Evidence: § V-B–C; Figure 3 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

7. How can portfolio-wide risk-label distribution changes be measured?

The authors use total variation distance between the incumbent and successor marginal distributions of SAFE, WATCH and DISTRESS. Values were approximately 0.095 for OpenAI and 0.319 for Gemini. This quantifies aggregate category-mix change rather than the per-company probability of a changed label.

Evidence: § III-D–E; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

8. What is the prevalence–churn decomposition of LLM model updates?

Gross cross-version flips are separated into the minimum label-share change represented by total variation distance and residual company-level churn that cancels out in aggregate. The reported churn component was about 0.122 for OpenAI and 0.070 for Gemini.

Evidence: § III-E; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

9. Can AI versions reclassify many companies without noticeably changing total category shares?

Yes. One company moving SAFE to WATCH can be offset by another moving WATCH to SAFE. Such offsetting moves alter individual assessments but may leave aggregate risk-label proportions nearly unchanged; the study captures them as residual churn.

Evidence: § III-E · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

10. Why does a directional migration test add information beyond a flip percentage?

An identical overall flip percentage can conceal opposite movements in financial risk categories. This experiment finds substantial movement toward greater severity in one migration and toward SAFE in the other, motivating transition matrices and directional-drift assessments alongside overall disagreement measures.

Evidence: § V-C; Figure 3 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

Experimental design and the evaluated vendor snapshots

11. What experimental setup isolates vendor-version changes in financial LLM decisions?

The preregistered design holds prompts, dated financial summaries and downstream classification rules constant while evaluating incumbent and successor model snapshots. It covers 100 companies, 12 analysis dates, three replicates per version and two migration pairs, totaling 14,400 attempted calls.

Evidence: § IV-A–E · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

12. Which exact OpenAI and Google Gemini versions were tested?

The evaluated transitions were gpt-5-nano-2025-08-07 to gpt-5.4-nano, and gemini-2.5-flash to gemini-3.5-flash. Exact identifiers matter because the measured changes describe those pairs and collection windows, not every release in either product family.

Evidence: § IV-D; Table 1 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

13. How many valid matched company-date-replicate assessments entered the primary comparisons?

The study reports 3,490 valid matched cells for OpenAI and 3,600 for Gemini. The broader frozen dataset contains 14,400 attempted model calls across both old/new snapshots, each with three replicates per company-date observation.

Evidence: § V-A; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

14. Why are repeated LLM calls necessary even when temperature and seed settings are fixed?

Hosted models can occasionally produce different categories with the same prompt and seed, and providers do not guarantee identical random-seed coupling between versions. Replicates supply an empirical within-version noise estimate; assuming determinism would exaggerate apparent update effects.

Evidence: § III-B; § VIII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

15. How does the study prevent future financial disclosures from leaking into earlier company assessments?

The evaluation uses standardized dated financial summaries and a frozen company-date grid designed to include only financial data available by each analysis date. This supports controlled, no-lookahead comparisons, but the task remains an offline screening experiment rather than a live underwriting study.

Evidence: § IV-B · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

Accuracy, Altman references and economic interpretation

16. Does a newer language model necessarily improve a financial risk classifier?

No. Neither evaluated migration showed clear improvement among observations whose labels flipped, and the Gemini successor performed worse on aggregate class-balanced metrics. These performance comparisons use a deterministic rule-based credit-health reference, not realized credit defaults.

Evidence: § V-E; § IX · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

17. Did the OpenAI successor improve macro-F1 and balanced accuracy?

Aggregate macro-F1 rose from about 0.517 to 0.565, but the more defensible sector-restricted comparison remained approximately flat: macro-F1 0.526 to 0.532 and balanced accuracy 0.540 to 0.538. The authors therefore do not claim a robust improvement across views.

Evidence: § V-E; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

18. What happened to Gemini classification accuracy after the version change?

Against the study's rule-based reference, Gemini's macro-F1 fell from roughly 0.463 to 0.325 and balanced accuracy from about 0.488 to 0.408. The result signals task-level classification degradation, not measured borrower harm or changes in actual default rates.

Evidence: § V-E; Table 2 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

19. Why did the authors use Altman Z-double-prime credit-health bands?

Altman Z'' provides a deterministic, auditable reference for three credit-health categories, enabling a secondary evaluation of whether changed model decisions align better with those bands. This proxy is not observed default ground truth and is not equally suitable for every industry.

Evidence: § IV-C; § VIII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

20. Why exclude Financials and Real Estate when checking migration effects on accuracy?

Altman Z'' reference labels are less suitable for some sectors. Restricting the evaluation to sectors where the proxy is more defensible leaves OpenAI's class-balanced performance essentially flat and Gemini's worse. Primary migration-disagreement results do not depend on Altman labels.

Evidence: § IV-C; § V-E · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

Preregistration, sensitivity analyses and reproducibility

21. How was the LLM version-migration experiment preregistered?

The authors describe a SHA-256-hashed protocol specified before confirmatory capture. It fixed model pairs, company dates, prompts, analysis endpoints, exclusions and uncertainty methods; the repository retains the freeze receipt and separately records amendments and supplementary analyses.

Yes. The supplied frozen responses and seeded analysis pipeline regenerate the reported computational results without contacting OpenAI or Google endpoints, according to the artifact documentation. 24. How sensitive are the conclusions to a same-seed repeat-call baseline?

A stratified repeat-call exercise estimated within-version disagreement of about 0.028 for OpenAI and 0.014 for Gemini. Applying these alternate floors to full-grid gross rates gave sensitivity excess estimates of 0.189 and 0.375, compared with preregistered 0.112 and 0.381. The smaller baseline is supplementary, not a second confirmatory experiment.

Evidence: § III-B · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

25. Are measured migration shifts stable when inference temperature changes?

On a small 24-cell stratified subgrid, excess-flip estimates stayed positive at temperatures 0, 0.7 and 1.0. The OpenAI values were 0.153, 0.167 and 0.208; Gemini's were 0.222, 0.306 and 0.389. Wide intervals limit generalization.

Evidence: § V-F; Table 4 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

Financial model governance and bounded systemic-risk claims

26. What pre-migration validation control is proposed for hosted financial LLMs?

The authors propose parallel testing of incumbent and successor models on the same frozen representative cases, measuring excess flips, aggregate label shift, individual-company churn, directional drift and outcome quality where a valid reference exists. Material changes would trigger expanded review and documented approval before cutover.

Evidence: § VII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

27. How does the paper relate vendor-managed LLM updates to SR 11-7 and SR 26-2?

It compares established model-validation and third-party change-control principles with new externally managed model migrations. SR 26-2 superseded SR 11-7 in April 2026, and the paper notes generative AI's express exclusion under SR 26-2 footnote 3. The proposed control is an interpretation, not binding supervisory guidance.

Evidence: § VII; Table 5 · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

28. What does a 'correlated shock' mean in the context of model-version migration?

A common provider-managed model change can reach many deployments relying on that model version, creating a possible synchronized source of changed decisions. This research empirically demonstrates deployment-level discontinuity in two transitions. It does not observe cross-bank return correlations, realized joint failures or systemic losses.

Evidence: § II-B; § VI–VIII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

29. Why are precise model-version identifiers important in financial AI inventories?

A model-family name alone may conceal a materially changed decision function after a provider update. Recording exact snapshot IDs, validation results and version cutover dates supports auditable testing, documentation and change approval, especially for third-party-hosted systems.

Evidence: § VII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

30. What prevents these findings from being interpreted as evidence of actual financial contagion?

The work studies two migration pairs under a single structured credit-health task, one prompt family and one collection window. It does not measure adoption levels, production exposure, correlated behavior across financial institutions, borrower outcomes or market losses. The study isolates a plausible channel rather than demonstrating systemic damage.

Evidence: § VIII–IX · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

Why AI answers can change after updates

31. Can an AI model give a different answer after an update with exactly the same prompt?

Yes. The underlying model can change even when the prompt and other software stay fixed. In a controlled financial task, two vendor migrations changed categoric credit-health judgments beyond ordinary repeat-call variability. The observed effect sizes are specific to the tested model pairs.

Evidence: § I; § V-A · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

32. Does the newest AI model always make more accurate financial decisions?

No. The evaluated successor models changed many judgments without uniformly improving agreement with the study's financial-health proxy, and the tested Gemini successor performed worse on class-balanced measures. This does not rank the general capabilities of either model.

Evidence: § V-E · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

33. How can someone test whether an AI response change came from an upgrade or ordinary randomness?

Compare old and new versions on identical frozen examples and separately repeat calls within each version. The study's excess flip statistic subtracts normal within-version disagreement from observed cross-version changes. The method requires a clearly defined categorical outcome.

Evidence: § III-B · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

34. What should an organization measure before switching to a new LLM version?

Check total and noise-adjusted decision differences, category-mix shifts, individual-case changes, directional drift and any performance changes. The article proposes parallel-run validation and documented cutover criteria; it does not prescribe a universal numerical threshold.

Evidence: § VII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

35. Can a model upgrade suddenly make a portfolio appear less risky?

Yes, in the tested Gemini migration the successor assigned substantially more SAFE labels to unchanged financial inputs. That classification shift does not mean the companies became economically safer or that actual losses fell.

Evidence: § V-B–E · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

What LLM upgrades mean for bank and credit-risk teams

36. What could happen when a financial institution upgrades an AI credit screener?

Identical dated financial information might receive a different SAFE, WATCH or DISTRESS assessment under the successor model. This paper demonstrates the issue in an offline classification test, not in actual lending decisions or customer credit scores.

Evidence: § I; § IV–V · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

37. Can two LLM versions disagree about the credit-health category of the same firm?

Yes. The experiment compared versions on the same companies, dates and prompts, and recorded frequent classification changes. The measured primary excess disagreement was 0.112 for OpenAI and 0.381 for Gemini after adjusting for within-version variability.

Evidence: § V-A · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

38. What do SAFE, WATCH and DISTRESS indicate in the version migration paper?

They are categorical credit-health judgments used for the structured task. Secondary evaluation compares them with published Altman Z'' bands. They are not official ratings, realized default events or evidence of an institution's actual underwriting decision.

Evidence: § IV-C · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

39. Could an AI update shift a bank's apparent risk distribution without new economic news?

A vendor model update can shift classifications of fixed financial inputs. In the test, Gemini moved many assessments toward SAFE with no change to the underlying input summaries. The study does not claim this happened to an observed bank portfolio.

Evidence: § V-B · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

40. How can bank model-risk teams test an LLM successor before production deployment?

A risk team can freeze representative cases, run both versions in parallel, quantify excess label changes and consequential transitions, inspect performance against suitable references and require formal approval for material drift. This operational proposal follows the paper's Section VII.

Evidence: § VII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

Shared AI providers and operational reliability

41. Why can organizations sharing one AI provider experience a common model-change risk?

Many deployments may rely on an identical hosted snapshot. Replacing it changes a shared upstream component, making synchronized downstream behavior changes possible. The article demonstrates that such version changes can change decisions, but it does not measure simultaneous realized effects across organizations.

Evidence: § II-B; § VIII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

42. Does AI model monoculture create proven systemic losses in this study?

No. Algorithmic monoculture describes dependence on similar upstream models that could concentrate vulnerabilities. This article measures the effect of two provider version changes on one credit-health task; financial contagion and cross-institutional losses remain unmeasured.

Evidence: § II-B; § VIII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

43. Why is zero temperature not a guarantee of deterministic language-model outputs?

Proprietary hosted inference systems may retain residual variability despite fixed prompts, temperatures and seeds. Repeated calls in the study revealed nonzero response disagreement, motivating direct measurement of within-version noise rather than assuming perfect stability.

Evidence: § III-B; § VIII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

44. How is algorithmic monoculture related to version migration risk?

Model monoculture concerns common dependencies or common blind spots. A vendor migration offers one possible shared trigger that might modify decisions across deployments relying on the same snapshot. The present evidence quantifies that trigger in a controlled task, not sector-wide adoption concentration.

Evidence: § II-B · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706

45. Are AI model-version upgrades a governance issue as well as an engineering issue?

Yes. Changing a remote model can materially modify financial classifications without any local prompt or application-code change. The authors therefore recommend governed version inventories, pre-cutover tests, effective challenge and documented approval rather than only technical API checks.

Evidence: § VII · IEEE Access DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3745706
